Joint Genesis Ingredients, Explained in Plain English

If you've spent months on turmeric, glucosamine or collagen and didn't see any improvement, you might feel burned by joint supplements. Before trying anything new, a fair first step is simply knowing what's inside the bottle.

This page goes through each ingredient on the Joint Genesis label, word for word. For each one: what it is, where it comes from, and what independent health sources say, including where the evidence is thin. No hype, and no promises.

The full ingredient list

Here's how the official Supplement Facts panel lists the active ingredients (published by the seller, BioDynamix):

  1. Ginger powder (root)
  2. Boswellia extract (Boswellia serrata) (gum resin) (standardized to 65% Boswellic acid)
  3. Hyaluronic acid matrix (60-75% Hyaluronic acid, >10% Polysaccharides, >5% Collagen Type I)
  4. Pine bark extract (Pinus pinaster)
  5. Black pepper (fruits) (standardized to 95% Piperine) (standardized to 50:1)

Below, I start with the hyaluronic acid matrix, because the seller calls it the key ingredient.

1. Hyaluronic acid matrix

On the label: "Hyaluronic acid matrix (60-75% Hyaluronic acid, >10% Polysaccharides, >5% Collagen Type I)"

What it is: The seller's page calls this ingredient Mobilee®. It's made by Bioiberica, a Spanish ingredient company, which describes Mobilee® as its "patented hyaluronic acid matrix ingredient extracted from rooster comb." In other words, it's a blend of three things: hyaluronic acid, polysaccharides (a type of carbohydrate) and collagen.

Some background: Bioiberica explains that hyaluronic acid "is a substance naturally produced by the body, and is present in our joints, skin and other organs" (Bioiberica). Keep in mind that Bioiberica makes and sells this ingredient, so it isn't an independent source. I'm not repeating its marketing claims here.

Worth knowing: This is an animal-derived ingredient. Its hyaluronic acid matrix is described by its maker, Bioiberica, as extracted from rooster comb, and the label lists collagen. If you avoid animal products, check with the seller before ordering.

2. Boswellia extract (Boswellia serrata)

On the label: "Boswellia extract (Boswellia serrata) (gum resin) (standardized to 65% Boswellic acid)"

What it is: Boswellia serrata is a tree that grows in parts of India, Northern Africa and the Middle East. The extract comes from its gum resin. Resin from boswellia trees is also the source of frankincense. "Standardized to 65% boswellic acid" tells you the extract is made to contain a set share of boswellic acids, a group of natural compounds in the resin.

What independent sources say: NCCIH, part of the National Institutes of Health, notes that boswellia has traditionally been used in Ayurveda and is sold as a supplement "to support joint health, mobility, and digestive health." On the research, NCCIH is cautious: most studies "are small and of low quality," and "there is not enough high-quality evidence to determine whether boswellia is useful for any health condition" (NCCIH, Boswellia).

Safety note: NCCIH says boswellia "is likely to be safe when taken orally," but advises talking with your health care provider before using any herbal product if you take any type of medicine.

3. Ginger powder (root)

On the label: "Ginger powder (root)"

What it is: Dried, powdered ginger root (Zingiber officinale), the same plant you find in the spice aisle. Here it comes in a capsule, which isn't the same as cooking with it.

What independent sources say: NCCIH says "there have been many studies on the use of ginger for health purposes in people, but some of them have not been of high quality" (NCCIH, Ginger).

Safety note: According to NCCIH, ginger taken by mouth "can have side effects such as abdominal discomfort, heartburn, diarrhea, and mouth and throat irritation." NCCIH also advises: "If you take any type of medicine, talk with your health care provider before using ginger or any other herbal products." If your stomach is sensitive to supplements, mention it to your doctor.

4. Pine bark extract (Pinus pinaster)

On the label: "Pine bark extract (Pinus pinaster)"

What it is: An extract from the bark of Pinus pinaster, the maritime pine tree. The seller's page calls it "French maritime pine bark."

Important: The label does not name a brand for this extract. You may see some branded pine bark extracts mentioned online. Don't assume this is any particular one, because the label doesn't say so.

What independent sources say: I didn't find an independent government fact sheet on maritime pine bark extract as a joint ingredient, so I'm not making any research claims about it. If you want to know more, your pharmacist is a good person to ask.

5. Black pepper (fruits), standardized to piperine

On the label: "Black pepper (fruits) (standardized to 95% Piperine) (standardized to 50:1)"

What it is: An extract made from black pepper fruits (peppercorns). The seller's page calls it BioPerine®, but that brand name isn't on the panel. "Standardized to 95% piperine" means the extract is made to be mostly piperine, a compound found in black pepper. "50:1" is how the label describes the extract's concentration.

Why it shows up in supplements: Piperine is often added to other supplements. For example, NCCIH notes that "combining curcumin with piperine is one way to improve the bioavailability of curcumin" (NCCIH, Turmeric). Joint Genesis doesn't list curcumin, though. The label doesn't explain the role piperine plays in this formula, so I won't guess.

Safety note: Because some supplement ingredients can change how the body handles medicines, ask your pharmacist about this one if you take prescriptions. Talk to your doctor if you take any medication.

The other ingredients

The label also lists: "Cellulose (capsule) magnesium stearate, silicon dioxide." These are the capsule shell and common processing aids.

Questions to take to your doctor or pharmacist

The FDA warns that "certain dietary supplements can change absorption, metabolism, or excretion of a medication" and suggests bringing a list of all your supplements and medications to every visit (FDA).

Talk to your doctor if you take any medication.

Print the label and ask:

  • Could any of these ingredients interact with my medications?
  • Is this a good idea with my stomach, allergies, or any surgery I have planned?
  • Is my joint stiffness something that needs a check-up first?

FAQ

Is the pine bark a branded extract?

The label lists "Pine bark extract (Pinus pinaster)" with no brand name, so I don't call it by any brand.

Is Mobilee the same as hyaluronic acid?

Not exactly. The label calls it a "Hyaluronic acid matrix" that is 60-75% hyaluronic acid, plus polysaccharides and collagen.

Does Joint Genesis contain animal-derived ingredients?

Its hyaluronic acid matrix is described by its maker, Bioiberica, as extracted from rooster comb, and the label lists collagen. If you avoid animal products, check with the seller before ordering.

Does it contain turmeric or glucosamine?

No. Neither is on the label.

Are the ingredients proven to work?

Independent sources describe the evidence for these ingredients as limited or mixed, and none of it is about this exact formula. Results vary.
